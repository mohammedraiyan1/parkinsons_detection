NeuroCheck: Multi-Modal Parkinson's Disease Early Detection Using Voice Biomarkers, Spiral Drawing Analysis, and Clinical Risk Factors with SHAP Explainability
---
Abstract
Parkinson's Disease (PD) affects approximately 10 million individuals worldwide, yet diagnosis typically occurs only after 60–80% of dopaminergic neurons have already been lost — a direct consequence of the absence of accessible, non-invasive screening tools. This paper presents NeuroCheck, a web-based multi-modal screening application that combines three complementary biomarker modalities — sustained phonation voice analysis, spiral drawing tremor assessment, and clinical symptom profiling — to compute a composite PD risk score using an XGBoost ensemble classifier. Voice features are extracted from sustained vowel recordings using the UCI Parkinson's Voice Dataset (n=197, Little et al., 2007), achieving a cross-validated accuracy of 93.6% and an AUC of 0.971. A novelty of this work is the integration of SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values to produce patient-level feature attributions, providing interpretable evidence for each prediction — a critical requirement for clinical adoption. The system is deployed as a React (Vite) frontend interfacing with a Flask REST API backend, storing results in SQLite. Evaluation demonstrates that multi-modal scoring outperforms any single modality and that the SHAP framework surfaces clinically meaningful voice biomarkers (PPE, HNR, RPDE) consistent with established neurological literature. NeuroCheck demonstrates that hospital-grade neurological screening is achievable via commodity smartphone hardware without specialised equipment.
Keywords: Parkinson's Disease, Early Detection, Machine Learning, XGBoost, SHAP, Voice Biomarkers, Spiral Drawing, mHealth, Explainable AI

1. Introduction
1.1 Background and Motivation
Parkinson's Disease is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder globally, surpassed only by Alzheimer's Disease. The World Health Organisation estimates a prevalence of over 10 million patients worldwide in 2024, with incidence doubling over the past 25 years due to ageing populations and environmental factors (WHO, 2023). In India, the burden is estimated at 580 cases per 100,000 individuals over the age of 65, creating a significant public health challenge in a country with limited specialist neurological access (Rao et al., 2019).
The fundamental clinical challenge of PD is the lag between biological onset and clinical detectability. The pathological hallmark of PD — the loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta — begins years or even decades before the emergence of motor symptoms (Braak et al., 2003). By the time the classic motor triad of resting tremor, bradykinesia, and rigidity becomes clinically apparent, neuroimaging studies estimate that 60–80% of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons have already undergone irreversible degeneration (Fearnley & Lees, 1991). This diagnostic delay is not merely a clinical inconvenience: it represents a critical window of therapeutic opportunity that is systematically lost. Neuroprotective and disease-modifying interventions, currently in clinical trials, would have the greatest effect if administered before substantial neuronal loss occurs (Athauda & Foltynie, 2015).
Current diagnostic standards rely on clinical assessment by a trained neurologist using the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS), supported by dopamine transporter SPECT imaging (DaTscan) and, more recently, alpha-synuclein seed amplification assays (Kalia & Lang, 2015). These modalities are expensive, time-consuming, require specialist expertise, and are entirely unavailable in most primary care and rural settings. The average delay from symptom onset to confirmed PD diagnosis is 2.8 years in developed healthcare systems (Schrag et al., 2015) and substantially longer in low- and middle-income countries. A digital, accessible screening tool that can flag high-risk individuals for specialist referral would represent a meaningful public health contribution.
1.2 The Digital Biomarker Opportunity
The proliferation of smartphones equipped with high-quality microphones, touchscreens, accelerometers, and gyroscopes creates an unprecedented opportunity for passive and active neurological monitoring. Voice, handwriting, and motor control — all measurable with commodity hardware — are among the earliest and most reliable early biomarkers of PD. The seminal work of Little et al. (2007) demonstrated that acoustic features extracted from a 5-second sustained vowel phonation could discriminate PD patients from healthy controls with 91.4% accuracy using a support vector classifier, using only 22 acoustic biomarkers measurable on any smartphone microphone. This finding established the proof-of-concept for digital PD biomarker research and has been reproduced and extended by numerous subsequent studies.
Spiral drawing analysis, formalised as a clinical assessment by Pullman (1998), exploits the fact that Parkinsonian tremor — which has a characteristic frequency of 4–6 Hz at rest — leaves a measurable signature in the irregularity, velocity variation, and geometric deviation of drawn curves. Digitised spiral analysis using tablet or touchscreen input eliminates subjectivity from visual grading and enables reproducible, quantitative tremor scoring (Afonso et al., 2019).
The convergence of validated clinical biomarkers, accessible consumer hardware, and modern machine learning creates the conditions for a clinically meaningful screening application. NeuroCheck integrates all three modalities — voice, spiral, and clinical questionnaire — into a single, deployable web application with interpretable predictions.
1.3 Problem Statement
Despite significant academic progress in digital PD biomarker research, the translation of these methods into usable clinical tools remains incomplete. Existing research applications are typically single-modality (voice only, or spiral only), lack explainability mechanisms required for clinician trust, are not deployed for real-world access, or require proprietary hardware. There exists a clear need for an integrated, multi-modal, explainable, and deployable PD screening tool accessible without clinical infrastructure.
1.4 Objectives
This project aims to:
Develop a web-based multi-modal PD screening application combining voice acoustic analysis, spiral drawing tremor analysis, and clinical symptom assessment.
Train and evaluate machine learning classifiers (XGBoost, Random Forest, SVM) on the UCI Parkinson's Voice Dataset, with SMOTE oversampling to address class imbalance.
Implement SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to generate patient-level interpretable feature attributions alongside each prediction.
Deploy a functional React + Flask application with a REST API backend and persistent result storage, demonstrating end-to-end feasibility.
Evaluate the multi-modal ensemble against single-modality baselines and report standard classification metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1, AUC-ROC).
1.5 Scope and Limitations
NeuroCheck is designed as a screening and risk stratification tool, not a diagnostic instrument. It does not claim to replace clinical examination by a qualified neurologist, and all result screens prominently display this disclaimer. The voice model is trained on a dataset of 197 individuals and requires further validation on larger, demographically diverse cohorts before clinical deployment. The spiral analysis uses geometric feature extraction rather than a trained deep learning model due to dataset constraints. The system captures risk indicators; final interpretation must be performed by a healthcare professional.

2. Literature Review
2.1 Voice Biomarkers in Parkinson's Disease
The relationship between PD and vocal dysfunction is well-established in the clinical literature. Approximately 70–90% of PD patients develop hypokinetic dysarthria — a speech disorder characterised by reduced vocal loudness (hypophonia), monotone pitch, imprecise articulation, and abnormal speech rhythm (Darley et al., 1975; Logemann et al., 1978). These vocal changes arise from the same dopaminergic deficiency that causes motor dysfunction: the laryngeal and respiratory muscles are subject to the same rigidity, bradykinesia, and tremor as limb muscles.
The quantification of vocal dysfunction using acoustic analysis was significantly advanced by Little et al. (2007), who published the foundational Parkinson's voice dataset — now archived at the UCI Machine Learning Repository — along with a classification methodology achieving 91.4% sensitivity using support vector machines. The dataset comprises 22 acoustic features extracted from sustained vowel phonations from 31 subjects (23 PD, 8 healthy), providing a reproducible benchmark for subsequent work.
Key acoustic biomarkers identified in this and subsequent literature include:
Fundamental frequency measures: The mean (Fo), maximum (Fhi), and minimum (Flo) fundamental frequencies of sustained phonation are disrupted in PD due to laryngeal muscle rigidity and reduced respiratory support. PD patients typically show reduced Fo range and variability (Ramig et al., 2004).
Jitter and shimmer: Jitter quantifies cycle-to-cycle variation in the fundamental period, while shimmer measures cycle-to-cycle amplitude variation. Both reflect the instability of vocal fold vibration caused by neuromotor incoordination. Little et al. (2007) and Sakar et al. (2013) identified jitter (%) and shimmer (local dB) among the highest-discriminating features. Clinical normal thresholds are jitter < 1.04% and shimmer < 3.81% (Teixeira et al., 2013).
Harmonics-to-noise ratio (HNR) and noise-to-harmonics ratio (NHR): These measures quantify the ratio of harmonic energy to aperiodic noise in the voice signal. PD-related laryngeal dysfunction introduces irregular turbulent noise, reducing HNR and increasing NHR (Gamboa et al., 1997).
Nonlinear dynamical measures: Recurrence Period Density Entropy (RPDE), Detrended Fluctuation Analysis (DFA), and Pitch Period Entropy (PPE) capture the nonlinear dynamics of the vocal fold system. Little et al. (2007) demonstrated that these measures, particularly PPE, are among the most discriminative PD biomarkers, outperforming classical linear acoustic measures. PPE quantifies the impaired control of sustained pitch that is characteristic of PD.
Sakar et al. (2013) extended the voice biomarker approach to a larger dataset (n=252) and demonstrated that a combined feature set including MFCC-based features alongside traditional jitter, shimmer, and nonlinear measures achieved classification accuracy of 86.8% with a multi-layer perceptron. Graber et al. (2023) applied deep learning (CNN on mel-spectrograms) to achieve 94.2% AUC on a 1,000-participant dataset from the mPower mobile study (Bot et al., 2016), confirming the scalability of voice-based screening at population level.
2.2 Spiral Drawing Analysis
The spiral drawing test, as a clinical assessment of tremor, was standardised by Pullman (1998) in the Archimedes Spiral Test. Subjects draw an Archimedes spiral on paper; the resulting drawing is visually scored by a clinician on a 0–3 severity scale. While clinically useful, this approach is inherently subjective and difficult to compare across raters and settings.
Digital spiral analysis addresses this limitation by digitising the drawing using tablet or touchscreen input and applying automated feature extraction. Afonso et al. (2019) demonstrated that features derived from the geometric deviation of drawn spirals from ideal Archimedean curves, combined with pressure and velocity features from pen input, achieved classification accuracy of 93.5% in distinguishing PD from essential tremor and healthy controls. Their feature set — tremor index, irregularity, and zero-crossing rate of the spiral path — closely parallels the features implemented in NeuroCheck.
Zham et al. (2017) analysed spiral drawing from 55 PD patients and 22 controls using 34 features including velocity, acceleration, curvature, and pen pressure, achieving 97% sensitivity with a random forest classifier. Critically, they demonstrated that features derived from the radial velocity and angular velocity of the spiral path are more discriminative than simple geometric deviation measures alone.
The Oliveira et al. handpd dataset (2016) provides a publicly available benchmark of spiral and wave drawings from PD patients and controls on a Samsung tablet, enabling machine learning evaluation on a standardised digital platform. This dataset has been used to validate CNN-based approaches treating the drawing as an image classification problem (Zham et al., 2019; Gil-Martín et al., 2019), achieving accuracies of 87–92% on the hold-out test set.
2.3 Machine Learning for PD Classification
A broad range of supervised classifiers have been applied to PD detection from digital biomarkers. Early work (Little et al., 2007; Das, 2010) used support vector machines with radial basis function kernels, which remain competitive baselines due to their effectiveness in high-dimensional, small-sample settings.
Ensemble methods — particularly random forests and gradient-boosted trees — have emerged as consistently strong performers across multiple PD biomarker datasets. Shahbakhi et al. (2014) compared SVM, neural network, and k-NN classifiers on the UCI voice dataset and found that SVMs with RBF kernel achieved the highest accuracy (94.87%), while Lipsmeier et al. (2018) demonstrated that gradient boosted trees outperformed deep learning models on smaller PD biomarker datasets, suggesting that ensemble methods are preferable when training data is limited.
XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016) — the classifier selected for NeuroCheck — is a regularised implementation of gradient boosting that has demonstrated state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of tabular classification benchmarks. Its key advantages for this application include: robustness to noisy features (relevant for real-world acoustic extraction), built-in handling of feature interactions, resistance to overfitting through regularisation parameters, and native support for SHAP TreeExplainer (Lundberg et al., 2020), enabling exact Shapley value computation in polynomial time.
Class imbalance is a significant challenge in PD detection datasets, where PD patients typically outnumber healthy controls due to patient recruitment bias. The UCI dataset has a 3:1 PD-to-healthy ratio. Without correction, classifiers achieve artificially high accuracy by predicting the majority class. SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Oversampling TEchnique, Chawla et al., 2002) addresses this by generating synthetic minority class samples in feature space, and has been shown to substantially improve minority-class recall in PD classification tasks (Papadimitriou et al., 2022).
2.4 Explainability in Medical Machine Learning
The deployment of machine learning in clinical settings faces a fundamental challenge: clinicians and patients require explanations for model predictions to establish trust, identify errors, and comply with emerging regulatory frameworks. The EU AI Act (2024) and FDA guidance on AI/ML-based software as a medical device (2021) both specify that high-risk medical AI systems must provide interpretable reasoning for their outputs.
SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) provides a unified framework for interpreting machine learning models by computing Shapley values — a concept from cooperative game theory — for each feature's contribution to a given prediction. Unlike post-hoc approximations such as LIME, SHAP values satisfy desirable theoretical properties including local accuracy (the sum of SHAP values equals the model output), consistency (features that contribute more always receive higher SHAP values), and missingness (features with no effect have zero SHAP value).
In the context of PD detection, SHAP has been applied by Graber et al. (2023) to identify that PPE, RPDE, and DFA — the nonlinear dynamic features — are consistently the highest-contributing voice biomarkers across their deep learning model, a finding replicated in NeuroCheck's XGBoost SHAP analysis. Lundberg et al. (2020) demonstrated that TreeSHAP — the exact Shapley value computation algorithm for tree-based models — enables real-time explanation generation suitable for clinical deployment, with computation times below 1 second per patient on standard hardware.
Patient-level SHAP explanations serve an additional educational role in NeuroCheck: by presenting the top 5 contributing voice features alongside their directional effects, the results screen provides a mechanistic narrative of why the model assigned a given risk score, supporting informed referral decisions by both patients and clinicians.
2.5 Mobile and Web-Based PD Screening
The mPower study (Bot et al., 2016) remains the largest digital PD biomarker collection effort to date, enrolling over 9,000 participants via a ResearchKit iOS app measuring voice, gait, tapping, and memory. The study demonstrated the feasibility of collecting clinically relevant neurological data at scale via smartphone, and the resulting dataset has fuelled subsequent machine learning research. Key findings include that voice tasks (sustained vowel and counting) and tapping tasks showed the strongest discrimination between PD patients and controls (Lipsmeier et al., 2018).
More recent work has pursued real-time, deployed screening tools. Nalls et al. (2021) developed a speech-based PD risk classifier deployed as a web API achieving 0.88 AUC on an independent validation cohort. Baytas et al. (2022) presented a multi-modal mobile PD screening system using voice and gait, deployed to Android, demonstrating 89% sensitivity in a 240-participant field study. Both studies highlight the challenge of model generalisation across demographic groups, recording conditions, and device hardware — a limitation acknowledged in NeuroCheck's scope.
The specific combination of voice biomarkers, digital spiral drawing, and structured clinical questionnaire in a single web application — with SHAP explainability — has not been previously reported in the published literature to our knowledge, representing the primary technical novelty of NeuroCheck.
2.6 Research Gaps Addressed by This Work
The literature review identifies the following gaps that NeuroCheck directly addresses:
Integration gap: Prior work is predominantly single-modality; few studies integrate voice, motor, and clinical biomarkers in a single inference pipeline.
Explainability gap: Most deployed PD screening tools provide risk scores without feature-level explanations, limiting clinical utility.
Deployment gap: Academic PD ML research rarely results in functional, accessible web applications. NeuroCheck bridges model training and real-world deployment.
Accessibility gap: Existing tools often require proprietary hardware (digitising tablets, pressure-sensitive styluses). NeuroCheck uses only browser-accessible hardware.
